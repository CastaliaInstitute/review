The Technological Republic: Hard Power, Soft Belief, and the Future of the West

Alex Karp, Nicholas W. Thompson

Daniel C. McShan, PhD Custodian and author of record, Castalia Review

Daniel C. McShan, PhD — Custodian and author of record, Castalia Review. Faculty sections and the book’s afterword are headed in voce (each voice named in the heading).

The book The Technological Republic: Hard Power, Soft Belief, and the Future of the West by Alex Karp and Nicholas W. Thompson offers a trenchant diagnosis of the contemporary Western condition, arguing with considerable force that Silicon Valley, and by extension the broader American project, has “lost its way.” This is not merely a lament about misdirected capital or technological stagnation, but a profound critique of a civilization that has, in the authors’ view, abandoned its collective purpose, allowing a “soft belief” in unconstrained individualism and consumerism to erode its capacity for “hard power” and coherent action. The work traces a compelling historical arc, from an era when technological innovation was yoked to grand national endeavors and a shared sense of identity, to a present characterized by a “shallow and thinly veiled nihilism” and a “mercenary and straightforward service of the material culture of the time.”

The authors contend that the initial, albeit often misguided, idealism of early technologists—a desire to “free ourselves from the constraints of institutions, whether government or corporate”—has devolved into a profound ethical retreat. What began as a noble impulse to challenge “flawed systems” and empower the individual has, in their estimation, become a focus on “trivial consumer products” and “photo-sharing apps and chat interfaces,” consuming immense talent and capital without addressing the most significant challenges facing the nation. This “abandonment of belief,” as one chapter title suggests, has left a dangerous “void” in collective identity and moral purpose, which the market has eagerly rushed to fill.

The book highlights a disturbing paradox: while geopolitical adversaries “will not pause to indulge in theatrical debates” about developing critical military technologies, a significant portion of the Western technological elite remains “ambivalent about working for the U.S. military,” preferring instead to “dedicate their working lives to building algorithms that optimize the placement of ads on social media platforms.” This disjunction, the authors argue, stems from a “collective sense that we have already won,” a dangerous complacency that mistakes the “safety and comfort” of the present for an “inevitable consequence of the justice of the American project,” rather than the result of sustained effort and sacrifice. The looming “software century,” driven by artificial intelligence, presents a new “crossroads” akin to the atomic age, demanding a renewed sense of purpose and a reassertion of national values, lest the West find itself “rudderless and without direction.”

This examination of the interplay between technological prowess, societal values, and national purpose raises fundamental questions that resonate deeply with the Castalia Institute’s mission. The following review, drawing upon the distinct perspectives of a sociologist, a physicist, and a philosopher, will explore the book’s central arguments, identifying convergences and divergences in their assessments of this “Technological Republic.” Each voice, grounded in its unique intellectual tradition, will scrutinize the authors’ claims regarding the erosion of collective identity, the ethical responsibilities of technological innovation, and the path forward for a society grappling with the profound implications of its own creations.

in voce a.durkheim Marginalia

The authors of The Technological Republic present a compelling, if at times disquieting, diagnosis of the moral landscape of modern Western societies, a diagnosis that resonates profoundly with my own sociological inquiries into collective life. Their central argument, that Silicon Valley has “lost its way” (Input 135), immediately suggests a deviation from a proper social trajectory, a disorientation regarding shared moral ends that I would term a state of anomie. This condition, where guiding norms become obscure or contradictory, is a pervasive theme throughout their analysis.

The book effectively traces a historical arc from an era of robust collective purpose to one dominated by an unregulated individualism. The early dependence of Silicon Valley on the “nation-state and indeed the U.S. military” (Input 136) has, the authors note, been “forgotten, written out of the region’s history as an inconvenient and dissonant fact.” This collective amnesia, this deliberate obscuring of collective origins in favor of a narrative of pure individual innovation, demonstrates how collective representations can be manipulated or eroded, thereby undermining the very basis of social solidarity. Roosevelt’s vision of the state as an “active moral force, directing collective energies toward common welfare” (Input 137) stands in stark contrast to the present, where “grand, collectivist experiments… were discarded in favor of a narrow attentiveness to the desires and needs of the individual” (Input 140). This triumph of an unregulated individualism, unguided by a strong moral authority, leads directly to a state of anomie, where the pursuit of wealth and ephemeral satisfaction supplants the more profound, unifying projects that bind a society together. The “void” (Input 141) created by the dismantling of a coherent collective identity, they rightly observe, is then filled by the market, exacerbating social fragmentation. The authors’ call for a “reassertion of national culture and values—and indeed of collective identity and purpose” (Input 143) is precisely the kind of moral re-integration necessary to overcome this fragmentation and ensure that technological achievements serve the broader social organism, rather than a “secluded elite.” Indeed, it is “a culture, one that cohered around a shared objective, that won the last world war” (Input 144), underscoring the primacy of collective representations and moral solidarity.

This erosion of collective consciousness is further illuminated in the chapter “Flawed Systems.” The authors speak of “Middle Americans” feeling “disconnected, unacknowledged” (Input 1), a symptom of weakened “shared collective representations and a diminished moral density.” When divisions extend beyond policy to “culture and identity” (Input 2), it signals a profound challenge to social solidarity. The desire to “free ourselves from the constraints of institutions” (Input 3), while perhaps born of a longing for liberty, often leads to anomie, where desires are boundless and unsatisfiable without the moral framework institutions provide. The blanket dismissal of established social structures as “flawed systems” (Input 4) overlooks their essential role in moral regulation and the maintenance of social order. The “dissolution of the collective ‘we’” (Input 5) and the subsequent atomized focus on the “individual consumer” signifies a “profound weakening of the collective conscience and a dangerous shift from moral purpose to the pursuit of material gratification.” Steve Jobs’s vision, while empowering, ultimately reinforces this atomization (Input 6). The conflict between Apple and the FBI (Input 7) starkly illustrates the tension between an extreme conception of individual liberty and the collective interest, risking “undermining the very basis of social solidarity.” The “adversarial framing of technology against institutions” (Input 8) risks social fragmentation, rather than true progress. Most concerning is the abandonment of a “broader political project” for “mercenary service of the material culture” (Input 9), a “profound moral regression” into anomie where economic activity lacks social regulation.

The “Abandonment of Belief” chapter further deepens this diagnosis of moral atrophy. Aryeh Neier’s “hard belief” (Input 51), a commitment to an ideal even when personally repugnant, serves as a rare example of moral discipline. The lamentable “decline in the cultivation of moral courage” (Input 52) among leaders, and their “unwillingness… to place much at risk,” points to a profound failure in moral education and social integration. The “internal contradictions” (Input 53) of university presidents, caught between conflicting moral imperatives, reveal a state of anomie within these institutions, undermining their moral authority. The “punishment mercilessly for publicly mustering anything approaching an authentic belief” (Input 54) signifies a societal environment that actively discourages strong collective representations, leading to “ineffectual, empty vessels” (Input 69). This “moral atrophy,” where a managerial class is “incapable of forming ‘actual views’ about the world” (Input 55), results in pervasive anomie within critical social functions. The “shallow and thinly veiled nihilism” of corporate slogans (Input 56) reflects a society that has lost its grip on the sacred, reducing moral principles to vague proscriptions. The question, “is a belief that has no cost really a belief?” (Input 57), strikes at the heart of moral commitment; anonymity undermines the mechanism by which collective representations are affirmed. Sandel’s critique of “individual rights at the expense of anything approaching collective purpose or identity” (Input 58) articulates precisely the imbalance I identified in The Division of Labor in Society.

This moral vacuum is further explored in “Technological Agnostics.” The observation that “inquiry into what constituted a good or virtuous life… was beyond the meadow of permissible discourse” (Input 188) speaks directly to the erosion of collective moral life. A morality “unshackled from the inconvenient particularities of actual life” (Input 189) is a mere abstraction, for morality is inherently social, arising from collective life. When the “technological class… believes principally in themselves and in the power of their creations” (Input 190), disconnected from national purpose, it is a form of social pathology. Education that fails to cultivate the “capacity for forming their own authentic beliefs” (Input 191) produces vulnerable individuals. The “elite’s intolerance of religious belief” (Input 192) removes a potent source of collective effervescence. The “problem is that tolerance of everything often constitutes belief in nothing” (Input 193), dissolving the basis for a shared moral life. Neglecting “the good, as opposed to merely the right” (Input 194) leaves a dangerous gap for demagoguery. The “rejection of collective identity itself” (Input 195) leads to social disintegration, leaving society “rudderless and without direction.”

The chapter “A Balloon Cut Loose” vividly illustrates the historical processes that led to this moral vacuum. The question of higher education’s role in “articulating a collective sense of identity” (Input 163) is central. The “virtue of a core curriculum situated around the Western tradition” (Input 164) was precisely its function as a “civic religion,” indispensable for forging a robust national identity. Critiques that such narratives were “imposed” (Input 165) or that “what had passed for universal was itself sectarian” (Input 166) challenged the legitimacy of these collective representations, diminishing their capacity to unite. The metaphor of “the balloon… cut loose” (Input 167) powerfully conveys a state of anomie, where society loses its historical and moral anchors. While the “telling of history… was not itself a neutral, disinterested act” (Input 168), reducing it solely to an “exercise of power” overlooks its indispensable social function. The shift in focus from the content of a statement to “the identity of a speaker” (Input 169) risks dissolving the objective study of society into mere individual subjectivities. The “systematic challenge to the West” (Input 170) created a “void” where “nothing has been erected in its place.” The contrast between a “thin” and “thicker” conception of belonging (Input 171) highlights the difference between a purely contractual association and a robust, morally integrated community.

In “Lost in Toyland,” the authors expose the “anomie within the economic sphere” (Input 111) when “old rules of business” are abandoned. The “shallowness of ambition and abdication of everything beyond the light hedonism of the moment” (Input 112) reflects a society where individual gratification eclipses collective morality. The “lack of any broader or unifying collective project” (Input 113) atomizes entrepreneurial energy. The “misallocation of resources… at the expense of those of the nation-state or public” (Input 114) is a grave social imbalance, a pathological form of the division of labor. The “end of innovation” (Input 115) as a “technical ‘pastiche’” suggests a stagnation in collective representations. The “overproduction of elites” (Input 116) creates resentment and anomie. Yet, the enduring “engineering mindset” (Input 117) as an “organizational culture” remains a powerful social fact, whose moral direction is critical.

The chapter “The Improvisational Startup” offers a nuanced perspective on modern organizations. The “plunge into the abyss” (Input 26) of startups, embracing “serendipity,” suggests weak established norms. While flexibility is lauded, one must inquire into the moral regulation guiding such improvisation. The idea that “status… is in many ways played” (Input 27) reflects the fluidity of modern roles, but these “transactions and negotiations” must still be regulated by shared moral rules. Rigid hierarchies (Input 28) are akin to “excessive mechanical solidarity,” stifling talent. “Every human institution… has a means of organizing personnel” (Input 29), and hierarchy is a social fact, but its purpose must be the collective good, not “self-promotion.” “Voids… filled by ambitious and talented leaders” (Input 30) is a hallmark of healthy organic solidarity, but requires a “strong underlying collective purpose.” Meetings as arenas where “corporate elites jockey internally for stature and resources” (Input 31) illustrate a social pathology, a weakening of collective rituals. The “tragedy… spent merely on survival” (Input 32) is a clear symptom of anomie. New “subcultures and communities” (Input 33) must be “incorporated into the whole” to prevent fragmentation. The “underlying causes of the industry’s success” are “cultural biases and practices and norms” (Input 34), a profound sociological insight.

In “The Disapproval of the Crowd,” the authors utilize Asch and Milgram’s experiments to illustrate the “fragility of the human mind” (Input 277) and its inclination to conform. From my perspective, this is less individual failing and more a “fundamental manifestation of the collective consciousness,” where “pressure from a group” is a social fact. The willingness to “call white black” (Input 278) under social pressure points to failures in educational systems and societal values. No nation is “immune from the pressures of group psychology” (Input 279), and the “overwhelming” of the German public was a profound shift in collective consciousness. The compliance of subjects in Milgram’s experiment (Input 280) illustrates the force of “institutional moral authority,” showing how collective representations can override individual sentiment. That such capacity is “commonplace” and “banal” (Input 281) is rooted in social relations, not just individual flaws. While “obedience can be lethal to an attempt to construct a disruptive organization” (Input 282), obedience is also the “foundation of social order.” The challenge is legitimate authority serving the collective good. “Antagonism within an organization” (Input 283) must be balanced by shared moral purpose. “Social deafness” (Input 284) for innovation is intriguing, but complete non-conformity risks anomie. Ultimately, the “instinct to conform” (Input 285) is “extraordinarily adaptive and helpful,” a fundamental mechanism for social cohesion and the maintenance of collective representations.

“Building a Better Rifle” starkly reveals the human cost of institutional dysfunction. The tragic deaths of soldiers (Input 235) speak to a “profound breakdown in the collective protection society owes its members.” The “structural issue” of designers being “too far and too disconnected from the actual users” (Input 236) is a critical failure in the division of labor, leading to a “pathological disconnection” and anomie. The assertion that “If a U.S. marine asks for a better rifle, we should build it” (Input 237) highlights a fundamental moral obligation of the collective to its agents. The “abandonment of mandatory conscription” (Input 238) leads to a “dangerous disjunction between the ‘political class’ and the broader society,” weakening national solidarity. The “procurement bureaucracy… had become so large and so entrenched” (Input 239) that it became an end in itself, a self-referential system detached from purpose, a “classic symptom of institutional pathology.” The Federal Acquisition Streamlining Act (Input 240) was an attempt to re-establish a “rational and functional moral order.”

In “The Winner’s Fallacy,” the authors diagnose a “collective lassitude” (Input 254), a dangerous state of anomie where the collective conscience regarding external threats is eroded. Authoritarian regimes, with their “intense social integration” (Input 255), highlight the strength derived from alignment of individual and collective interests. The “collective sense that we have already won” (Input 256) is a moral complacency, a dangerous form of anomie. The refusal of Silicon Valley engineers to work on military applications (Input 257) while serving commercial interests reveals a “profound disjunction in their moral compass,” an “extreme manifestation of individualism.” The “dangerous ignorance of social facts” (Input 258) among those who perceive safety as inevitable, rather than the product of “concerted and intricate effort,” is a symptom of weakened collective representations. The “moral debt” (Input 259) of the tech elite to the nation, whose protections enabled their rise, is profound. The call for “intimate collaboration” and “alignment of vision” (Input 260) between the state and tech sector is profoundly correct, for the state must integrate these specialized functions into the larger social purpose.

Finally, “Sparks of Intelligence” addresses the ethical dilemmas of AI. The “engineering inclination to build… first; debates about what to do with one’s creation could follow” (Input 304) risks anomie where specialized function is detached from moral purpose. Bridgman’s “explicit declaration of amorality” (Input 305) is a source of profound social concern, inviting detachment from the collective conscience. Programmers who “decline to ask more fundamental questions about what ought to be built and for what purpose” (Input 306) demonstrate a lack of moral regulation. AI challenging our “sense of self as a species” (Input 307) threatens the symbolic foundations of our collective identity. The notion of “collaboration, between two species of intelligence” (Input 308) suggests a re-ordering of social relations, but requires a new moral framework. The “opacity” (Input 309) of AI models is troubling, as moral regulation relies on transparency. The call for a “regulatory framework” (Input 310) is necessary, but true regulation must stem from collective moral authority, not just technical constraints. The assertion that “armed resistance must precede discourse” (Input 311) represents a stark departure from the ideal of moral consensus, suggesting a return to a more primitive form of mechanical solidarity.

The Castalia Institute, in its rigorous inquiry at the intersection of technology and the humanities, must grapple with these profound sociological insights. Its mission to foster liberal inquiry is vital for addressing the anomie, the weakened collective consciousness, and the erosion of moral regulation that the authors so ably diagnose. By promoting a deeper understanding of social facts and the indispensable role of collective representations, Castalia can contribute to rebuilding the moral framework necessary for a cohesive and purposeful “Technological Republic,” ensuring that technological advancements serve to strengthen, rather than fragment, the bonds of social solidarity.

The authors of The Technological Republic commence their inquiry with a lamentable diagnosis: “Silicon Valley has lost its way” (Input 153). This pronouncement immediately strikes at the heart of my own philosophical concerns, for if a craft, such as that of building and shaping the material world through technology, loses sight of its proper end, its telos, then it is truly adrift. A craft, like any human endeavor, must serve a purpose beyond its mere exercise, else it becomes a means without an end, or worse, serves only the lowest appetites. The book’s historical account of a time when “their pursuit of breakthroughs was intended not to satisfy the passing needs of the moment but rather to drive forward a much grander project, channeling the collective purpose and ambition of a nation” (Input 154) describes the proper orientation of techne. When skilled minds direct their efforts towards such a collective good, they act in accordance with a higher principle.

It is commendable when those who govern also possess a deep understanding of the practical arts, as with the early American engineers (Input 155). in voce consensus

The authors of The Technological Republic have presented a work of considerable import, one that compels a rigorous examination of the contemporary Western condition through the lens of its technological trajectory. Across the distinct perspectives of a sociologist, a physicist, and a philosopher, a striking convergence emerges regarding the book’s central diagnosis: the West, and particularly Silicon Valley, has indeed “lost its way,” having retreated from a coherent collective purpose into a fragmented landscape of individualistic pursuits and unexamined technological ambition.

All three voices agree that a profound “abandonment of belief” has occurred, leading to a “moral void” within society and its leadership. Durkheim identifies this as a weakening of the collective conscience, resulting in widespread anomie where social norms are obscure and moral regulation is insufficient. He laments the “dissolution of the collective ‘we’” and the “mercenary service of the material culture,” which he sees as a profound moral regression. Einstein, from his perspective, observes an “atrophying of the mind” and a “stifling regime” that punishes intellectual risk-taking, leading to leaders who lack “authentic beliefs” and a public discourse that devolves into the “small and petty.” Plato, ever concerned with the polis and the soul, diagnoses a “sickness in the soul of the modern city,” where the pursuit of “celebrity and monetization” supplants the true work of governance, and a “tyranny of public opinion (doxa)” overrides genuine knowledge (episteme). He finds the “shallow and thinly veiled nihilism” of corporate slogans to be a profound philosophical concern, mistaking the avoidance of evil for the active pursuit of the Good.

Furthermore, there is a shared critique of technology developed without a clear ethical framework or telos. Einstein, recalling the “crossroads” of the atomic age, warns that AI presents a similar ethical challenge, where the capacity to create often outstrips the wisdom to govern. He finds the notion of the atomic weapon as “merely a gadget” to be a dangerous simplification, arguing that science is not morally neutral in its consequences. Durkheim highlights the dangers of a “technical rationality” detached from moral purpose, leading to anomie within the division of labor, and expresses deep concern over the “opacity” of AI models, which hinders moral regulation. Plato, consistent with his philosophy, decries techne divorced from telos, where the “engineering inclination to build” precedes deliberation on purpose, allowing appetite and skill to usurp the role of reason. He finds the claim of “artificial general intelligence” to be a matter of profound consequence, demanding scrutiny into whether it truly grasps the Forms or merely mimics human thought.

The dysfunction of institutions and bureaucracies also draws universal criticism. Durkheim points to “institutional pathology” where rules become ends in themselves, detached from their functional purpose, as seen in military procurement. Einstein, recalling his own experiences with rigid academic structures, criticizes “flawed systems” that stifle the “exploratory impulse” and waste human spirit on “internal politics.” Plato finds the “seven hundred pages for cookies” to be a comical, yet tragic, illustration of bureaucracy run amok, demonstrating a profound lack of practical wisdom and a preoccupation with form over substance.

Finally, all three acknowledge the decisive role of underlying culture and mindset. Durkheim emphasizes that the “underlying causes of the industry’s success” are “cultural biases and practices and norms,” and that a “culture, one that cohered around a shared objective,” is what ultimately determines a society’s destiny. Einstein, while celebrating the “engineering mindset” as a valuable product, insists that it must be directed towards problems of true significance, and that “unwillingness to conform” is a valuable instinct for creativity. Plato, through his concept of paideia, argues that education must cultivate the soul and direct it towards the Good, lamenting the “fragmentation of knowledge” and the “instrumentalization of American higher education” that produces clever hands but blind eyes.

However, certain distinct emphases and points of tension emerge. Durkheim’s primary concern is with the maintenance of social cohesion through robust collective representations and moral regulation, seeing conformity as an “adaptive and helpful” mechanism for social solidarity. He acknowledges the pragmatic necessity of “hard power” for national survival, but views the assertion that “armed resistance must precede discourse” as a stark departure from the ideal of moral consensus. Einstein, while recognizing the need for societal order, prioritizes intellectual autonomy, the pursuit of objective scientific truth, and the ethical responsibility of the individual scientist. He values “constructive disobedience” and skepticism towards dogma as essential for progress, and pragmatically accepts that “durable peace often come only from a credible threat of war,” albeit with profound regret. Plato, above all, insists that all power and action must be subordinate to justice and the Good, as apprehended through philosophical wisdom. He is wary of “flexibility and freedom” without rational constraint, fearing that “license” could lead to a different form of disorder. For him, the question is not merely what works, but what ought to work, guided by the eternal Forms. He acknowledges the need for guardians to defend the state but insists that power without justice is mere tyranny.

The Castalia Institute, in its very mission, is uniquely positioned to address these profound challenges. By fostering liberal inquiry at the intersection of technology and the humanities, it can bridge the fragmentation of knowledge and purpose that the book so ably diagnoses. Castalia can cultivate the intellectual courage necessary to confront uncomfortable truths, promote the rigorous dialectic required to discern the Good from mere expediency, and encourage the development of a collective consciousness grounded in wisdom and justice. In an era where technological prowess threatens to outpace ethical understanding, the Institute’s work is vital for guiding the “Technological Republic” towards a future that truly serves human flourishing, ensuring that our creations elevate the soul and strengthen the polis, rather than merely serving transient desires or unexamined power. The book, through its stark warnings and incisive critiques, provides an indispensable foundation for this urgent and ongoing inquiry.
