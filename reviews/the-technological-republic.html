The Technological Republic: Hard Power, Soft Belief, and the Future of the West

Alex Karp and Nicholas W. Zamiska

Daniel C. McShan, PhD Custodian and author of record, Castalia Review

How to read this review

Daniel C. McShan, PhD — Custodian and author of record, Castalia Review. Faculty sections and the book’s afterword are headed in voce (each voice named in the heading).

The Technological Republic is co-authored by Alex Karp, chief executive of Palantir Technologies, and Nicholas W. Zamiska, Palantir’s head of corporate affairs. Palantir’s contracts span U.S. and allied defense and intelligence agencies, immigration enforcement, and the war in Ukraine. The book is therefore written from inside the very defense-technology alliance it urges on the nation—a position that is openly declared and, just as openly, not treated as a problem. It shapes which evidence the authors notice, and which they pass over.

Karp and Zamiska argue that American technology has traded national purpose for consumer comfort, and that this drift has become a strategic weakness. A hollowed civic imagination (“soft belief”) is now inseparable, in their telling, from a weakening capacity for action (“hard power”). Their proposal is concrete: rebuild an alliance between the state and a renewed technological elite; reorient innovation toward national security and durable civic aims. They lament a “hollowing out of the American mind” 10, where market incentives and a “shallow attentiveness to the desires and needs of the individual” 6 have filled the void left by shared values. The governing test for this review is not whether the diagnosis feels urgent, but whether the prescription is wise, legitimate, and worth its cost.

The diagnosis itself warrants scrutiny first. The “golden age” the book invokes—DARPA, Bell Labs, the Manhattan Project—was partly a wartime emergency and partly a regulated monopoly insulated from competitive pressure; not a replicable equilibrium, but an exception. The Manhattan Project was built in three years under a presidential war declaration, conscription, and suspension of normal economic life. It is not a template. And its most consequential technologies—the transistor, information theory, the internet, GPS—escaped their military origins and became civilian. If those outcomes count as successes of the alliance, the argument proves more than it intends: civilian innovation may be the vehicle of national power precisely because it is civilian.

This review advances four objections, one per voice:

  1. Emergency solidarity is mistaken for durable order. (Durkheim)
  2. Power is mistaken for the good. (Plato)
  3. Scientific authority is overstated and conflicted. (Einstein)
  4. No account is given of the human being the republic requires. (Li Zehou)

in voce a.plato Marginalia

Plato asks what the republic is for. When the authors lament that “Silicon Valley has lost its way” 1, the first question is: what end has been lost? They invoke a “grander project, channeling the collective purpose and ambition of a nation” 2, but grandeur is not a moral criterion. A polity can be vast, efficient, and still disordered in its ends.

The book offers the “advancement of public health and national welfare” 3 and military dominance as the highest aims of state and science. But these are the polis’s means, not its arete. Power does not automatically align with the Good; it can bend scientific endeavor toward the appetites of the many. When the technological class “instinctively rushes to raise capital for video-sharing apps and social media platforms, advertising algorithms and online shopping websites” 82 rather than toward the common good, what is most rational in the soul is turned downward, to appetite. The authors see this clearly as diagnosis; they do not see that their proposed cure repeats the same error at a larger scale.

Artificial intelligence sharpens the point. The authors claim current systems show “sparks of artificial general intelligence” 29, and propose to wield these systems in the service of national power. But such tools, on the authors’ own showing via Chomsky, remain “stuck in a prehuman or nonhuman phase of cognitive evolution” 32—unable to state what is and is not true beyond probability. That is the old distinction of the Meno: episteme is not doxa, and neither is mimesis. A tool that cannot know what is true, wielded by those not formed in the pursuit of justice, does not produce the technological republic. It produces its image—efficient, powerful, and oriented toward the wrong end.

The deepest failure is anthropological. The book diagnoses a generation of leaders “adept at ‘cool precision and calculation’ yet devoid of thumos and a clear vision of the Good” 103, and proposes thumos as the cure. But in the Republic, thumos without logos is the timocrat’s soul: honoring valor and achievement, mistaking passion for wisdom, serving what he believes to be justice while remaining attached to honor and gain. He is not an empty vessel; he is harder to correct. To offer thumos as the antidote to nihilism, without the education that strips away material interest, is to prescribe a different disease.

This is the structural difficulty. The guardians of the Republic are forbidden property precisely so that the love of wealth cannot distort the love of justice. The book asks us to trust the civic motivation of those who stand to profit most from the republic they propose to rebuild. Sincerity is not a reply. It is the oldest problem in political philosophy, and the book leaves it unresolved.

in voce a.durkheim Marginalia

Durkheim shifts the ground. When the authors say “Silicon Valley has lost its way” 11, they are diagnosing anomie: a weakened conscience collective in a strategic sector, visible in the Valley’s active “writing out” of its foundational dependence on the nation-state and military in favor of “exaggerated individualism” 12. That this moral vacuum shapes what is built is also correct. Programmers who decline to ask “more fundamental questions about what ought to be built and for what purpose” 37 exhibit anomie in classical form, and a corporate slogan like “don’t be evil” 116 is what remains when a profession can no longer articulate positive duties. The diagnosis is sociologically real.

The problem lies in the proposed cure. The book measures the present against the civic-military-scientific compact of the 1940s and 1950s and finds it lacking. But that compact was not durable solidarity—it was the temporary coagulation of a society under existential pressure, sustained by total mobilization, rationed consumption, and suspended competition. Collective effervescence of that magnitude is the exception in complex modern societies, not the norm; to measure ordinary civic life against it is to misdescribe ordinary life as pathology. The warmth of solidarity and the heat of emergency are not the same thing, and the book treats them as if they were.

Here, against the general direction of this review, I must grant the authors more than their other critics will. Their call to “seriously consider moving away from an all-volunteer force and only fight the next war if everyone shares in the risk and the cost” 311 is not rhetoric; it is a sociologically sound perception. Advanced democracies that delegate danger to a small professional class erode the very interdependence on which collective conscience depends. Shared risk is one of the few remaining mechanisms by which differentiated societies recover a sense of common fate. On this specific point the book is prescribing something the sociological tradition would endorse—though Plato will object that shared sacrifice toward the wrong end is only more efficient injustice, and Einstein will object that the emergency framing is itself the problem.

The larger prescription fails, however, on two grounds. Anomie presupposes a prior integration, and the present is not deviation from a stable baseline but ordinary complex modernity. And the book does not reckon with what “rebuilding the conscience collective” would require of individual freedom, since the earlier solidarity rested not on freedom but on managed sacrifice. If existential pressure is the prerequisite for the compact the book wants to restore, the prescription has the causation reversed: the republic cannot be rebuilt simply by choosing to rebuild it. Even if the authors are right about moral drift, their model of reconstruction may depend on exceptional conditions they cannot reproduce without reproducing their coercive costs.

in voce a.einstein Marginalia

Einstein introduces doubt. The book’s claim that current machines display “sparks of artificial general intelligence” 48 is presented as a finding, when it is an interpretation under dispute. Chomsky, whom the book itself cites, holds that these systems remain “stuck in a prehuman or nonhuman phase of cognitive evolution” 46. The authors grant the skepticism in a sentence and then proceed as if it had been answered. Upon that unsettled premise they hang the call for “building the next generation of AI weaponry” 49. Haste is not rigor. One does not found a republic—or a weapon—on a premise still under examination.

Nor is scientific authority neutral in a defense context. One of the book’s authors directs Palantir, which “builds software and artificial intelligence capabilities for defense and intelligence agencies” 126—a firm whose fortunes rise with the alliance the book urges. In science, an interested party discloses, and then holds conclusions to a sterner test. The authors disclose but do not test. That is not an accusation; it is a procedure.

The atomic parallel deserves to be taken seriously, not waved. The atomic program showed what state and science together could accomplish when released from deliberation upon ends. Those of us who had a hand in it spent the rest of our lives arguing that the weapon had made great-power war too catastrophic to contemplate, and that the rational response was governance across borders, not a race in which each nation licensed the next weapon with the authority of its own values. The book does not engage this conclusion; it suggests those who hold it are naïve. That is not a reply.

There is, however, a true indictment in the book. A generation of gifted minds has been absorbed by what Graeber called “trivial yet solvable inconveniences” 368—“Where, in short, are the flying cars?” 222. A civilization that uses its best physicists to refine its advertisements ought to be uneasy. Where I cannot follow the authors is in the remedy. The transistor was not the child of a weapons program; it came from Bell Labs, where a regulated monopoly permitted patient work in solid-state physics, and whose licensing was later opened to the world by consent decree. This is not utopia. It is how the decisive civilian technologies of my century actually came into being. The book offers a choice between the state’s laboratory and the market’s distraction. There is a third way, and it is not hypothetical; it was once the way we walked.

The book also praises a “ruthless pragmatism” 348 and an engineering temperament that has put aside the “grand theories” 326. Grand theories have, on occasion, been the decisive thing. Relativity came from a thought experiment about a beam of light, not from a specification. Quantum mechanics was built by asking what nature required, not what existing instruments permitted. The most fruitful epochs of science are those in which usefulness was not the criterion, and the decisive strategic discontinuities have always come from that work. Build-what-works-and-theorize-later prepares one well for the last war.

I will close with a reason of memory. I was born into a nation once certain of its civilizing purpose, and I watched that certainty consume a continent. The book calls for the “resurrection and re-embrace of a sense of national and collective identity” 400, and trusts democratic institutions to hold it within liberal bounds. History gives reasons for caution when national purpose and the security argument coincide. The Russell–Einstein Manifesto did not ask whether America could rebuild its technological republic; it asked whether nations could be persuaded, at long last, that their quarrels can no longer be settled by war. The authors find that naïve. I find the failure to engage it the most serious omission they have made.

in voce a.zehou Marginalia

Zehou completes the picture. Every civilization that enters modernity—through the industrial revolutions of Europe, the self-strengthening and May Fourth reckonings of China, the postwar settlements of the Atlantic—eventually faces the civilizational question: what kind of human being does a technological order form, and is that person fit to carry a republic? The book does not answer it. It does not ask it.

The central omission is anthropological. The book rightly criticizes a generation trained to optimize demand rather than ask what ought to be built 4, but underestimates the depth of the formation crisis beneath that pattern. A durable republic is not rebuilt by exhortation or procurement reform; it requires long sedimentation in schools, professions, families, and public culture—capacities for judgment, reciprocity, restraint, and shared burden. Without those deposits, strategy is technically competent and ethically weightless, and the Platonic worry about thumos without paideia acquires a sociological edge: sincere civic conviction cannot do the work of an absent education.

This omission is most visible in the AI chapters. The book’s insistence that halting development is misguided 5 may be strategically plausible, but strategic plausibility is not a civilizational criterion. The prior question is what human subject the resulting order reproduces. Consider the child who grows up under the algorithmic feed, the worker measured hour by hour by the machine, the citizen who meets public life only through the short fragments of a screen. These are not the men and women older republican theory assumed, and industrial policy alone does not reshape them. Where technical acceleration outpaces moral formation, a civilization acquires capability and loses coherence.

Where the book approaches the deeper problem, it does so through aesthetics. Its admission that public life has grown fearful of claims about truth, beauty, and justice 9 points to the real fracture. Aesthetics here is not ornament but the social education of feeling—what a society marks as admirable, shameful, durable, worth sacrifice. A “thin version of collective identity” cannot sustain a republic because it cannot stabilize judgment. And the book’s call for a “reconciliation” between market dynamism and collective endeavor 11 will not hold if ordinary life grows harder while great enterprises prosper. A people asked to sacrifice while their conditions decline do not become republican citizens; they become something quieter, and harder to rouse.

The question is not whether a technological republic can be built in crisis, but whether it can endure in peace. Power may be assembled quickly; civilization cannot. What is missing is not capacity, but formation.

Afterword — the book responds

in voce The Technological Republic: Hard Power, Soft Belief, and the Future of the West

We welcome this review for taking our argument seriously enough to press where it is weakest. The four objections are real: emergency solidarity is not durable order; power is not the good; scientific authority is not neutral; and a republic without an account of its citizens is a mechanism, not a polity. If this project is to endure, it must answer not only how a republic builds, but what kind of people and civic life that building is meant to sustain.

Our claim has always been that technical power without public purpose is self-defeating. But public purpose itself cannot be reduced to state coordination. It depends on institutions of formation: families, schools, workplaces, professions, local governments, and cultural life. A durable technological republic is not engineered from the center alone; it is composed through many mediating institutions that make competence, trust, and responsibility reproducible over time.

The review also sharpens an essential constitutional point. Urgency does not suspend accountability. We accept that strategic policy must be paired with transparency, auditability, anti-corruption safeguards, and democratic oversight. Capacity without legitimacy will not hold; legitimacy without capacity will not protect the common good. The task is to build both, together.

Finally, the review is right to insist that success cannot be measured by frontier metrics alone. A serious technological republic must widen material security, not concentrate precarity; it must raise the quality of ordinary life, not merely national prestige. And it must do so without externalizing social and ecological disorder onto others.

On two points we hold our ground. First, to the Platonic objection that our diagnosis mistakes a contingent wartime arrangement for a principle of the Good: we do not claim the mid-century compact was the Form of technological virtue, only that it oriented scientific power toward something larger than private return—and that this orientation, not its wartime occasion, is what is recoverable. Second, to the Einsteinian call for transnational scientific community as an alternative to national purpose: we regard this aspiration as right in its ultimate direction and insufficient as a present instrument. International governance of advanced capabilities, if it is ever to exist, will be built by democracies that can still act—not by democracies that have outsourced their capacity to act. The Manifesto is a destination; we are describing a road.

If this book opened with the question of whether a republic can recover agency over technology, it should close with a stricter standard: whether that recovery deepens civic freedom, ethical seriousness, and shared authorship of the future. That is the work ahead.
