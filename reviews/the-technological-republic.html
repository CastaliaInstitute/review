The Technological Republic: Hard Power, Soft Belief, and the Future of the West

Alex Karp and Nicholas W. Thompson

Daniel C. McShan, PhD Custodian and author of record, Castalia Review

Daniel C. McShan, PhD — Custodian and author of record, Castalia Review. Faculty sections and the book’s afterword are headed in voce (each voice named in the heading).

The Technological Republic arrives with considerable institutional weight. Alex Karp is the chief executive of Palantir Technologies, a surveillance and defense-intelligence software firm whose contracts extend across NATO militaries, border enforcement agencies, and the ongoing war in Ukraine; Nicholas Thompson is the president of the Atlantic Council’s Cyber Statecraft Initiative and a former editor of WIRED. The book draws openly on Karp’s operational experience at Palantir, and this institutional position—simultaneous advocate and commercial beneficiary of the defense-tech alliance the book recommends—is not incidental to the argument. It shapes which evidence the authors notice and which they pass over.

The provocation is about ends as much as means: what should advanced societies do with their scientific and organizational power, and what forms of belief can still justify collective sacrifice? Karp and Thompson argue that American technology has traded national purpose for consumer comfort, and that this drift has strategic costs. In their telling, a hollowed civic imagination (“soft belief”) is now inseparable from a weakening capacity for action (“hard power”). The book’s proposal is concrete: rebuild an alliance between the state and a renewed technological elite, and reorient innovation toward national security and durable civic aims. It laments a “hollowing out of the American mind” 10, where market incentives and a “shallow attentiveness to the desires and needs of the individual” 6 fill the void left by shared values. The governing test for this review is straightforward: not whether the diagnosis feels urgent, but whether the prescription is wise, legitimate, and worth its cost.

The diagnosis warrants scrutiny before the prescription does. The “golden age” the book mourns—DARPA, Bell Labs, the Manhattan Project—was partly a wartime emergency and partly a regulated monopoly insulated from competitive pressure, not a stable or replicable social equilibrium. The internet, GPS, and semiconductor fabrication all originated in defense funding and then escaped it; today’s decisive capabilities in AI, logistics, and communications are overwhelmingly civilian and commercial. Whether the right response is a return to the state-tech alliance, or an honest account of how civilian innovation already generates strategic power, is a prior question the book declines to ask. The voices below test that question from four angles: Plato on ends, Durkheim on social solidarity, Einstein on evidence, and Li Zehou on civilizational formation.

Taken together, they ask a single question: can technical capacity be rebuilt without hollowing out the human and civic substance a republic depends on?

in voce a.plato Marginalia

The authors begin with the lament that “Silicon Valley has lost its way” 1. The core question is straightforward: what end has been lost, and is the proposed national project ordered to the good or merely to scale and power? They invoke an earlier “grander project, channeling the collective purpose and ambition of a nation” 2, but grandeur is not itself a moral criterion. A polity can be vast, efficient, and still disordered in its ends.

The book offers the “advancement of public health and national welfare” 3 and military dominance as the highest aims of state power and scientific endeavor. But are these the ultimate arete of the polis, or merely its means? Power and prestige do not automatically align with the Good; they can bend scientific endeavor toward transient desires and the appetites of the many. The authors themselves observe a “narrow attentiveness to the desires and needs of the individual” 6, where “the market rewarded shallow engagement with the potential of technology.” This is precisely the danger named in the Laws: private gain, unchecked, corrodes the unity of the polis. When the technological class “instinctively rushes to raise capital for video-sharing apps and social media platforms, advertising algorithms and online shopping websites” 82 rather than the defense of the common good, the soul’s most gifted parts are misdirected. The authors’ own phrase—a “massive redirection of resources, both intellectual and financial, to sating the often capricious needs of capitalism’s consumer culture” 83—describes a condition in which the cultivation of virtue, and of a just polis, becomes nearly impossible.

Artificial intelligence sharpens the question. The authors claim current systems show “sparks of artificial general intelligence” 29; this compels us to ask what knowledge and creation properly are. A machine that produces works which “move millions” and express “beauty or truth” 30 may only imitate the expressions of a soul without possessing one. The authors themselves cite Chomsky’s counter, that such programs are “stuck in a prehuman or nonhuman phase of cognitive evolution” 32, unable to state what is and is not true beyond probability. That is the line I drew in the Meno: episteme is not doxa, and neither is mimesis. To abandon “grand theories and overarching belief structures to build, indeed often build anything, as long as it worked” 106 is to produce a world efficient but unguided by the Good—a world in which the “illusion of the good life” 206 is mistaken for the thing itself.

The Platonic concern then sharpens to a structural one. The book proposes to deploy these instruments of sophisticated mimesis in the service of national power, directed by those with a material stake in the outcome. A tool that cannot know what is true, wielded by those not formed in the pursuit of justice, does not produce the technological republic. It produces its image—efficient, powerful, and oriented toward the wrong end.

The book critiques the “abandonment of belief” 104 among modern leaders—“ineffectual, empty vessels” “adept at ‘cool precision and calculation’ yet devoid of thumos and a clear vision of the Good” 103. This is a failure of paideia: an education that no longer cultivates civic duty or the pursuit of the common good. A republic cannot thrive when its leaders are blown by the shifting winds of doxa rather than anchored by wisdom. The authors rightly name a “collective and contemporary fear of making claims about truth, beauty, the good life, and indeed justice” 428 that leaves only a “thin version of collective identity.” That is the condition of the many in the cave, mistaking shadows for reality.

Yet the proposed remedy—restoring authentic conviction in a “founding generation” of gifted builders—does not obviously produce the philosopher-king. In the Republic, thumos without the education of logos is the timocrat’s soul: honoring valor and achievement, mistaking passion for wisdom, serving what he believes to be justice while remaining attached to honor and gain. He is not an empty vessel; he is harder to correct. The book offers thumos as the antidote to nihilism, but leaves unanswered whether thumos, unformed by an education that strips away material interest, is a cure or a different disease.

The proposed “reconstruction of a technological republic” 383 that re-embraces a “sense of national and collective identity” is compelling. Yet the book repeatedly identifies this with “hard power,” including “AI weaponry” 9, and with a “ruthless pragmatism” 327 willing to suspend moral character for strategic advantage. The argument in these passages often confuses national greatness with the good. The unresolved question is whether the “durable peace” 59 the book invokes rests upon justice or upon the “credible threat of war” and terror 78—and whether one author’s leadership of a firm that “builds software and artificial intelligence capabilities for defense and intelligence agencies” 106 does not quietly determine which of the two is taken as the answer.

Yet one must press beyond the question of ends to the question of origins, for here the book’s deepest philosophical error becomes visible. The authors invoke the mid-century defense-science compact as the archē of technological purpose—the founding moment whose spirit the present has abandoned. But archē, properly understood, is not a historical episode; it is the principle from which all particular instances derive their goodness. To treat a contingent arrangement, produced by wartime emergency and shaped by the particular pressures of survival, as the ideal from which the present has deviated is to mistake a copy for an original Form. This is precisely the error I examined in the Meno: the man who can identify instances of virtue but cannot say what virtue is has doxa about a particular time, not episteme of the universal Good. He cannot guide recovery, because what he proposes to recover was itself only an approximation.

Consider what the book’s own exemplar reveals. The architects of that era’s most consequential project spent the remainder of their careers attempting to constrain, through international governance, what national purpose had made possible—because they had come to recognize that power exercised without justice tends toward catastrophe. The book invites us to recover a spirit. The question it declines to ask—whether that spirit was itself oriented toward the Good, or merely toward the power that emergency had temporarily made necessary—is the question on which everything depends.

The book’s most revealing moment is not its diagnosis but its proposed cure: a founding generation of technically gifted builders, oriented toward national defense by a renewed paideia. In the Republic, the philosopher-king is distinguished from the tyrant not by his competence or his patriotism but by a long education that deliberately strips away material interest—the guardians are forbidden property precisely so that the love of wealth cannot distort the love of justice. The book asks us to trust the civic motivation of those who stand to profit most from the republic they propose to rebuild. That is not a criticism one can answer by pointing to sincerity. It is the oldest problem in political philosophy, and it remains here unresolved.

in voce a.durkheim Marginalia

The authors open with the claim that “Silicon Valley has lost its way” 11—sociologically, a diagnosis of weakened conscience collective within a strategic sector. When a collective enterprise once animated by national purpose loses its moral compass, the condition is anomie: a normlessness in which the shared sentiments that regulated conduct have dissolved. The book documents this in the active “writing out” of the Valley’s foundational dependence on the nation-state and military, a selective memory that denies the social origins of its own success in favor of an “exaggerated individualism” 12.

The historical account of Roosevelt’s intent to “spur the scientific community forward in service of… public health and national welfare” 13 illustrates the state’s role as a moral agent, integrating individual effort into a shared project. In that period, scientific work was framed as a “natural extension of the national project” 15. The contemporary Valley, by contrast, has “strayed significantly from this tradition,” shifting toward the “consumer market” and the “desires and needs of the individual” 16. That shift fosters egoistic individualism and weakens social bonds in the technological sphere. The “void” left by dismantled collective structures and the failure to “resurrect anything substantial, a coherent collective identity or set of communal values” 17 has, as the authors note, been “filled by the market.” Still, the baseline itself must be examined: collective effervescence is a sociological phenomenon, not a normative ideal. Its wartime generators—total mobilization, rationed consumption, suspended competition—are part of the story. The warmth of solidarity and the heat of emergency are not the same thing, and the book often treats them as if they were.

This moral vacuum extends to what is built. Programmers “driven by the needs of capitalist culture, and to enrich itself,” decline to ask “more fundamental questions about what ought to be built and for what purpose” 37. That is anomie in classical form: the collective conscience fails to regulate economic activity. A technological elite’s “disinterest in the messiness and moral complexity of geopolitics” 91 exposes a disjunction in the division of labor—interdependence without shared duty. The “shallow and thinly veiled nihilism of a corporate slogan such as ‘don’t be evil’” 116 marks the same failure: professional groups unable to articulate positive duties or collective purposes.

The book’s account of the decline of “Western Civilization” curricula illustrates the same erosion. Such a curriculum, framed as a “civic religion, tethered largely to truth and history” 158, functioned as a collective representation binding a national identity. Its dismantling—and the “overextension” that made “the identity of the speaker” weigh more than what is said 164—leaves only a “thin conception of belonging” 166: rights and economics without moral density. Yet the book treats the displaced curriculum as if it carried objective moral authority. In my analysis, collective representations derive their force from the intensity of collective investment, not from metaphysical truth. Whether “Western Civilization” as taught expressed a genuine conscience collective, or a particular class’s successful universalization of its own perspective, is the question the book does not raise. A curriculum’s erosion does not by itself show that the solidarity it named was as broad as it appeared.

The book’s critique of government procurement shows the same pathology from the institutional side. When those designing military software are “too far and too disconnected from the actual users” 305, and when it is an “absurdity” to hesitate before giving better equipment to soldiers 308, the division of labor has lost its moral integration. The authors’ call to “seriously consider moving away from an all-volunteer force and only fight the next war if everyone shares in the risk and the cost” 311 directly addresses that loss: shared sacrifice rebuilds the collective conscience that otherwise dissolves.

The book calls, rightly, for a “reconciliation of a commitment to the free market… with the insatiable human desire for some form of collective experience and endeavor” 443. That is the modern problem in a single sentence: how to sustain social bonds amidst deepening individual differentiation. The authors correctly identify Silicon Valley’s success as driven by “cultural biases and practices and norms” 264—social facts, not hardware. But the emergent ‘organic solidarity’ they describe, with its “shadow hierarchies” and “lack of organizational legibility” 260, may foster ambition while generating new anomie if no broader collective conscience integrates it.

The book’s concluding call—“directing that engineering instinct, an indeed ruthless pragmatism [sic], toward the nation’s shared goals, which can be identified only if we take the risk of defining who we are or aspire to be” 338—names the sociological task precisely: reconstructing a conscience collective through shared purpose, revised collective representations, and renewed civic education. In principle, such reconstruction is not inconceivable; societies have rebuilt normative frameworks through new institutions and the slow reestablishment of professional ethics. But two unresolved problems remain. First, the baseline: anomie presupposes a prior integration, and the civic-military-scientific compact of the 1940s and 1950s may have been less a stable solidarity than the temporary coagulation of a society under existential pressure. Collective effervescence is the exception in complex modern societies, not the norm; to measure the present against it is to misdescribe ordinary life as pathology. Second, the cost: the book does not reckon with what “rebuilding the conscience collective” would require of individual freedom, since the earlier solidarity rested not on freedom but on managed sacrifice.

So the deeper sociological question is whether a conscience collective adequate to the book’s ambitions can be deliberately engineered, or whether it only crystallizes under conditions no policy can produce. The historical record leans toward the latter. If existential pressure is the prerequisite, the prescription has the causation reversed: the republic cannot be rebuilt simply by choosing to rebuild it. This is not a case for passivity; it is an insistence that the social facts be named accurately before the remedy is prescribed. Even if Karp is right about moral drift, his model of reconstruction may depend on exceptional conditions he cannot reproduce without reproducing their coercive costs.

in voce a.einstein Marginalia

The authors begin by observing that “Silicon Valley has lost its way” 1. The question one ought to ask of such a claim is simple: by what measure, and compared against what? The book takes as its measure the defense-science compact of the mid-twentieth century—DARPA, Bell Labs, the Manhattan Project—and treats the present as a falling-away. I was close enough to that period to remember that its most consequential fruits escaped the military ground that nourished them. The transistor, information theory, the laser became the foundation of civilian life. GPS, the internet, semiconductor fabrication: defense at origin, global infrastructure at maturity. If these outcomes are counted as triumphs of the state-science alliance, the argument proves more than it intends—civilian innovation may be the vehicle of national power precisely because it is civilian. One must be careful not to mistake a preferred chapter of history for a law of nature.

On the claim that current machines display “sparks of artificial general intelligence” 48: the book offers this as though it were a finding, when it is an interpretation under dispute. Chomsky, whom the book itself cites, holds that these systems remain “stuck in a prehuman or nonhuman phase of cognitive evolution” 46. The authors grant the skepticism in a sentence and proceed as if it had been answered. Upon such an unsettled premise they then hang the call for “building the next generation of AI weaponry” 49. I have watched, in my time, what it costs when the urgency of the state runs faster than the patience of knowledge. Haste is not rigor. One does not found a republic—or a weapon—on a premise that is still being examined.

The book offers the atomic age as a self-evident parallel. I cannot accept it so easily. I know what that parallel meant and what it cost. The atomic program showed what the union of state and science could accomplish when released from deliberation upon ends—and those of us who had a hand in it spent the rest of our lives arguing that the weapon had made war between great powers too terrible to contemplate, and that the only reasonable answer was to govern such capabilities together across borders, not to run a race in which each nation licensed the next weapon with the authority of its own values. The book does not engage this conclusion except to suggest that those who hold it are naïve. It is not a reply to call a warning naïve.

And one must be willing to name what interests the book serves. One of its authors directs Palantir, which “builds software and artificial intelligence capabilities for defense and intelligence agencies” 126—a firm whose fortunes rise with the very alliance the book calls for. The authors present this as a source of insight. It is also, unavoidably, a shaper of vision. A man does not easily see what would cost him if he saw it. This is not an accusation; it is a fact about human nature, and it is the reason we have learned, in science, to state our interests openly and to hold our conclusions to a sterner test when our livelihood leans upon them. To apply that standard here is not to settle the argument. It is the beginning of taking it seriously.

There is, however, a true indictment in the book. A generation of gifted minds has been absorbed by what Graeber called “trivial yet solvable inconveniences” 368—and he was right to ask, “Where, in short, are the flying cars?” 222. A civilization that uses its best physicists to refine its advertisements ought to be uneasy. On this the authors see clearly. Where I cannot follow them is in the remedy. It does not follow that the cure is military contract. The transistor was not the child of a weapons program; it came from Bell Labs, where a regulated monopoly permitted patient work in solid-state physics, and whose licensing was later opened to the world by consent decree. This is not a utopia I am describing—it is how the decisive civilian technologies of my century actually came into being. The book asks us to choose between the state’s laboratory and the market’s distraction. There is a third way, and it is not hypothetical; it was once the way we walked.

In “A Cloud or a Clock” the authors praise a “ruthless pragmatism” 348 and an engineering temperament that has put aside the “grand theories” 326 in favor of what functions. I would remind them that the grand theories have, on occasion, been the decisive thing. I once imagined myself riding alongside a beam of light; the imagining, not the apparatus, opened general relativity. Quantum mechanics was built by men asking what nature required, not by men asking what their instruments would permit. The most fruitful epochs of science have been those in which usefulness was not the criterion, and it is precisely there that the great strategic discontinuities have always come. An intellect impatient with grand theories polishes the architectures it already possesses. It does not discover what no one has yet thought. A doctrine of build-what-works-and-think-afterwards is a recipe for arriving at the last war very well supplied.

I must end in a more personal register. I was born into a nation that was once certain of its civilizing purpose, and I watched that certainty consume a continent. I do not speak as a cosmopolitan in love with an abstraction; I speak as a man who learned, too late, what national conviction in alliance with the security argument can do. The book calls for the “resurrection and re-embrace of a sense of national and collective identity” 400, and it trusts democratic institutions to hold that identity within liberal bounds. I have reasons of memory for being less certain.

The answer I came to, after Hiroshima, was not national purpose. It was something older and more demanding: that science is not a possession of states but a community across them, held together by what the evidence teaches rather than by the passport one carries. The Russell–Einstein Manifesto did not ask whether America could rebuild its technological republic. It asked whether nations could be persuaded, at long last, that their quarrels can no longer be settled by war. The authors of this book find that naïve. I find the failure even to engage it the most serious omission they have made.

in voce a.zehou Marginalia

Plato asks after the Good; Durkheim after solidarity; Einstein after evidence. The remaining question is civilizational: what kind of human being does a technological order form, and is that person fit to carry a republic? Every major civilization, in its long passage through modernity, has had eventually to answer this. There is something admirable in a work that refuses decadence. In a period trained in irony and habituated to managed decline, this book insists that history still demands organized will. Its opening claim that “Silicon Valley has lost its way” 1 is strongest when read as a diagnosis of ends rather than of sectors: it asks whether technical labor can once again be ordered to public purpose rather than dispersed across private appetite.

Its strongest conceptual move is to reject technological neutrality. Recalling the earlier integration of state machinery and scientific ambition for “public health and national welfare” 2, the argument recognizes that technology is always socially embedded: it reorganizes institutions, incentives, and horizons of action. That strength becomes a weakness when historical comparison hardens into programmatic nostalgia. The task is not to restore an emergency settlement but to construct legitimacy under ordinary civic conditions 3—the same boundary Durkheim raises against a wartime baseline, now turned toward the question of human formation.

The central omission is anthropological. The book rightly criticizes a generation trained to optimize demand rather than ask what should be built 4, but underestimates the depth of the formation crisis beneath that pattern. A durable republic is not rebuilt by exhortation or procurement reform alone; it requires long sedimentation in schools, professions, families, and public culture—capacities for judgment, reciprocity, restraint, and shared burden. Without those deposits, strategy is technically competent but ethically weightless, and the Platonic worry about thumos without paideia acquires a sociological edge: even sincere civic conviction cannot do the work of an absent education.

This omission is most visible in the AI chapters. The insistence that halting development is misguided 5 may be strategically plausible, but strategic plausibility is not a civilizational criterion—and the evidentiary gap Einstein identifies reappears here as an anthropological one. The prior question is what kind of human subject, and what rhythm of daily life, the resulting order reproduces. Consider the child who grows up under the algorithmic feed, the worker measured hour by hour by the machine, the citizen who meets public life only through the short fragments of a screen: these are not the men and women older republican theory assumed, and industrial policy alone does not reshape them. Where technical acceleration outpaces moral formation, a civilization acquires capability and loses coherence.

Where the book approaches this deeper problem, it does so through aesthetics. Its admission that public life has grown fearful of claims about truth, beauty, and justice 9 points to the real fracture. Aesthetics here is not ornament or elite taste but the social education of feeling—what a society marks as admirable, shameful, durable, and worth sacrifice. A “thin version of collective identity” cannot sustain republican continuity because it cannot stabilize standards of judgment.

There is also the institutional question, which must be named plainly. The capacity of a civilization is not commanded from above; it is laid down, slowly, through schools, professions, local government, and the quiet trust of neighbors in one another. The book itself names the needed “reconciliation” between market dynamism and collective endeavor 11, but such reconciliation will not hold if ordinary life grows harder while great enterprises prosper. A people asked to sacrifice while their daily conditions decline will not become republican citizens; they will become something quieter, and harder to rouse.

The final test is therefore twofold. Can a society rebuild its strategic competence? In principle, yes. Can it do so while also forming the kind of human being, and the kind of common life, that a republic requires to endure? That is the harder task, and here the book remains incomplete. Its closing call for renewed collective identity 12 is necessary, but it is not yet sufficient. Power is almost always rebuilt faster than civilization; where that is allowed to happen, the technological republic will be formidable, and still unformed.

The four voices converge. Plato presses the question of ends: does the proposed republic aim at the Good, or only at power? Durkheim presses the question of conditions: can solidarity be constructed without the coercive emergency that produced its mid-century model? Einstein presses the question of evidence: does strategic urgency outrun what is actually known? Li Zehou presses the question of formation: can the institutions and persons a republic requires be cultivated under ordinary civic conditions, or only mobilized in extraordinary ones?

On each axis, The Technological Republic is persuasive as a diagnosis of elite drift and civic thinning, and under-argued as a program of reconstruction. It is more convincing as a warning than as a guide.

Its strongest pages name a real problem; its weakest pages treat urgency as argument and scale as legitimacy. The afterword that follows gives the book a direct chance to answer these objections on its own terms.

Afterword — the book responds

in voce The Technological Republic: Hard Power, Soft Belief, and the Future of the West

We welcome this review for taking our argument seriously enough to press where it is weakest. The four voices identify distinct and genuine deficits—normative (Plato), sociological (Durkheim), scientific (Einstein), and civilizational (Li Zehou)—that any politics of technological capacity must answer if it is not to be reduced to strategy, competition, and production. If this project is to endure, it must address not only how a republic builds, but what kind of people and civic life that building is meant to sustain.

Our claim has always been that technical power without public purpose is self-defeating. But public purpose itself cannot be reduced to state coordination. It depends on institutions of formation: families, schools, workplaces, professions, local governments, and cultural life. A durable technological republic is not engineered from the center alone; it is composed through many mediating institutions that make competence, trust, and responsibility reproducible over time.

The review also sharpens an essential constitutional point. Urgency does not suspend accountability. We accept that strategic policy must be paired with transparency, auditability, anti-corruption safeguards, and democratic oversight. Capacity without legitimacy will not hold; legitimacy without capacity will not protect the common good. The task is to build both, together.

Finally, the review is right to insist that success cannot be measured by frontier metrics alone. A serious technological republic must widen material security, not concentrate precarity; it must raise the quality of ordinary life, not merely national prestige. And it must do so without externalizing social and ecological disorder onto others.

If this book opened with the question of whether a republic can recover agency over technology, it should close with a stricter standard: whether that recovery deepens civic freedom, ethical seriousness, and shared authorship of the future. That is the work ahead.
