The Technological Republic: Hard Power, Soft Belief, and the Future of the West

Alex Karp, Nicholas W. Thompson

Daniel C. McShan, PhD Custodian and author of record, Castalia Review

Daniel C. McShan, PhD — Custodian and author of record, Castalia Review. Faculty sections and the book’s afterword are headed in voce (each voice named in the heading).

The modern age, with its marvels of swift communication and intricate contrivances, presents a paradox: never before have men possessed such power to shape their world, yet rarely has there been such confusion as to the proper ends of this power. The work before us, The Technological Republic: Hard Power, Soft Belief, and the Future of the West, by Alex Karp and Nicholas W. Thompson, grapples with this very dilemma, offering a trenchant diagnosis of the West’s perceived decline and a provocative prescription for its renewal. The authors contend that the American technological sector, once a vital partner in grand national endeavors, has strayed from its purpose, seduced by the fleeting desires of the consumer market and a reluctance to engage with the harsh realities of geopolitical competition. This shift, they argue, has left the West vulnerable, its “soft belief”—a moral and cultural agnosticism—undermining its capacity for “hard power.”

The book’s central project is to awaken its readers to the urgent necessity of forging a renewed alliance between the state and the technological elite, one capable of directing innovation towards national security and a more robust collective identity. It laments a “hollowing out of the American mind” Marginalia — a.plato #10, where a lack of shared values has created a void, filled by market forces and a “shallow attentiveness to the desires and needs of the individual” Marginalia — a.plato #6. The authors call for a return to a more purposeful technological enterprise, one that understands its debt to the polis and is willing to confront the “grim reality of an ongoing geopolitical struggle for power” Marginalia — a.durkheim #19. They draw parallels to the atomic age, suggesting that we stand at a similar crossroads, where the ethical implications of powerful technologies, particularly artificial intelligence, demand a clear moral compass and a decisive will.

However, the very nature of this proposed “technological republic” invites profound questions from diverse perspectives. Does the pursuit of national advantage, however urgent, truly align with the highest Good for humanity? Can a society, having abandoned its “coherent collective identity or set of communal values” Marginalia — a.plato #10, simply re-embrace one, or must it first undertake a deeper moral and intellectual regeneration? And what of the ethical responsibilities of those who wield such immense technological power? These are not mere academic quibbles but fundamental inquiries into the soul of the modern state and the future of human flourishing, questions that demand a rigorous examination from philosophical, sociological, and scientific viewpoints. The following voices will explore these tensions, each bringing their own distinct lens to bear upon the authors’ compelling, yet often challenging, arguments.

in voce a.platoMarginalia

The authors open their discourse with a lament that “Silicon Valley has lost its way” Marginalia — a.plato #1, a potent image, indeed. But what, precisely, is this “way” that has been forsaken? Is it merely a deviation from a prior mode of operation, or a departure from a true telos—a proper end or purpose—for human endeavor? They speak of an earlier “grander project, channeling the collective purpose and ambition of a nation” Marginalia — a.plato #2, yet one must inquire: is this project truly grand, aiming at the Good, or merely grand in its scale and power? The distinction is crucial, for a city, like a soul, can be led astray by pursuing what is merely vast or expedient, rather than what is truly just and virtuous.

The book suggests that the “advancement of public health and national welfare” Marginalia — a.plato #3 and military dominance are the highest aims to which state power and scientific endeavor should be directed. Yet, are these the ultimate arete for the polis, or merely means to a more profound flourishing? Does the state’s “power and prestige” necessarily align with the pursuit of truth and the Good, or might it corrupt the scientific endeavor, bending it to serve transient desires or the appetites of the many? The authors themselves note a “narrow attentiveness to the desires and needs of the individual” Marginalia — a.plato #6 in the modern technological sphere, where “the market rewarded shallow engagement with the potential of technology.” This echoes precisely the danger I explored in the Laws, where the pursuit of private gain can undermine the unity of the polis, fostering faction and neglecting the common good. When the “technological class instinctively rushes to raise capital for video-sharing apps and social media platforms, advertising algorithms and online shopping websites” Marginalia — a.plato #82, rather than the defense of the common good, it signifies a profound misdirection of the soul’s most gifted parts. Indeed, the authors lament a “massive redirection of resources, both intellectual and financial, to sating the often capricious needs of capitalism’s consumer culture” Marginalia — a.plato #83, a condition that renders the cultivation of virtue and a just polis nearly impossible.

The advent of artificial intelligence, which the authors claim demonstrates “sparks of artificial general intelligence” Marginalia — a.plato #29, compels us to re-examine the very nature of knowledge and creativity. Can a machine truly apprehend the Forms of stability or arrangement, or does it merely offer a sophisticated imitation of human reasoning, derived from countless examples, much like a skilled artisan might imitate a form without truly understanding its essence? If a machine can create works that “move millions” and express “beauty or truth” Marginalia — a.plato #30, does this imply it possesses a soul, or merely imitates the expressions of one? The authors themselves cite Chomsky’s skepticism, who argues that such programs are “stuck in a prehuman or nonhuman phase of cognitive evolution” Marginalia — a.plato #32, lacking the human capacity for stating what is and is not true, beyond mere probability. This distinction is vital, for it separates true knowledge (episteme) from mere opinion (doxa) or sophisticated mimicry (mimesis), a distinction I explored in the Meno. To abandon “grand theories and overarching belief structures to build, indeed often build anything, as long as it worked” Marginalia — a.plato #106 is to risk building a world that is efficient but ultimately unguided by the Good, a world where the “illusion of the good life” Marginalia — a.plato #206 is mistaken for reality.

The book rightly critiques the “abandonment of belief” Marginalia — a.plato #104 among modern leaders, who are described as “ineffectual, empty vessels” Marginalia — a.plato #104 lacking “authentic belief” Marginalia — a.plato #104. This condition, where leaders are “adept at ‘cool precision and calculation’ yet devoid of thumos and a clear vision of the Good” Marginalia — a.plato #103, is a profound failure of paideia—an education that neglects to instill civic duty and the pursuit of a higher common good. How can a republic thrive when its leaders are so easily swayed by the shifting winds of doxa, rather than being anchored by wisdom? The authors lament a “collective and contemporary fear of making claims about truth, beauty, the good life, and indeed justice” Marginalia — a.plato #428, leading to a “thin version of collective identity” Marginalia — a.plato #428. This is precisely the condition of the many in the cave, mistaking shadows for reality, fearing to turn their gaze towards the true sun. To lose the capacity to “discern and indeed judge” Marginalia — a.plato #427 what is truly good and beautiful is to condemn the polis to drift aimlessly.

The proposed solution, a “reconstruction of a technological republic” Marginalia — a.plato #383 that re-embraces a “sense of national and collective identity,” is compelling. Yet, the book often conflates this with the pursuit of “hard power,” including “AI weaponry” Marginalia — a.plato #9, and a “ruthless pragmatism” Marginalia — a.plato #327 that justifies overlooking moral character for strategic advantage. One of the authors, being publicly associated with Palantir, a firm that “builds software and artificial intelligence capabilities for defense and intelligence agencies” Marginalia — a.plato #106, presents a perspective shaped by direct involvement in these very choices. This direct interest, while offering insight, also shapes the understanding of the telos of technological development and its relationship to national security. The question remains: can a “durable peace” Marginalia — a.plato #59 truly arise from the “credible threat of war,” or from terror Marginalia — a.plato #78, rather than from justice and wisdom? The “Leviathan” Marginalia — a.plato #85 of state power, without the guiding hand of the philosopher-king, can lead to tyranny, not true flourishing.

The book’s call to “harness and channel the creative energies of this new founding generation, these technical iconoclasts, into serving something more than their individual interests” Marginalia — a.plato #431 is admirable. But this “harnessing” must be achieved through proper paideia that cultivates arete and directs the soul toward the common good, rather than allowing its spirited and appetitive parts to dominate. To entrust leadership solely to those with a “stake in their own success” Marginalia — a.plato #357 risks elevating the love of wealth above reason and justice. The true measure of a polis is not merely its power or its material prosperity, but its capacity to cultivate virtuous citizens and to pursue the Good in all its endeavors. Without this guiding vision, even the most advanced technologies risk becoming mere instruments for unexamined desires, leading the city, and the souls within it, further astray.

For Castalia Institute, the profound questions raised by The Technological Republic are central to our mission of liberal inquiry. We must relentlessly pursue the true telos of technology, asking not merely what can be built, but what ought to be built, and for what ultimate good of the polis. Our task is to foster the dialectical engagement necessary to discern truth from opinion, justice from mere expediency, and to cultivate the wisdom and courage required to guide our technological age towards genuine human flourishing, rather than allowing it to drift aimlessly in a sea of unexamined desires and “moral agnosticism” Marginalia — a.plato #432.

in voce a.durkheimMarginalia

The authors commence with the assertion that “Silicon Valley has lost its way” Marginalia — a.durkheim #11, a statement that, from a sociological perspective, immediately suggests a weakening of the conscience collective within this vital sector. What does it signify for a collective enterprise, once animated by national purpose, to lose its moral compass? It points directly to a state of anomie, a normlessness where the shared sentiments and moral regulation that once guided its endeavors have dissolved. This is further evidenced by the active “writing out” of its foundational dependence on the nation-state and military, a selective collective memory that denies the social origins of its success in favor of an “exaggerated individualism” Marginalia — a.durkheim #12.

The historical account of Roosevelt’s intent to “spur the scientific community forward in service of… public health and national welfare” Marginalia — a.durkheim #13 illustrates the state’s essential function as a moral agent, integrating individual efforts into a coherent social project. Such a period, where scientific pursuits were a “natural extension of the national project” Marginalia — a.durkheim #15, must have been animated by a powerful collective effervescence and shared moral ideal. However, the modern incarnation of Silicon Valley has “strayed significantly from this tradition,” shifting its focus entirely to the “consumer market” and the “desires and needs of the individual” Marginalia — a.durkheim #16. This fosters an egoistic individualism, where private gratification supplants the collective good, leading to a profound weakening of social bonds and a pervasive anomie in the technological sphere. The “void” left by the dismantling of old collective structures and the failure to “resurrect anything substantial, a coherent collective identity or set of communal values” Marginalia — a.durkheim #17 has been, as the authors rightly observe, “filled by the market.”

This moral vacuum extends to the very purpose of technological creation. The authors lament that a generation of programmers, driven by “the needs of capitalist culture, and to enrich itself,” declines to ask “more fundamental questions about what ought to be built and for what purpose” Marginalia — a.durkheim #37. This is a classic description of anomie, where the collective conscience fails to regulate economic activity, leading to social disarray. The detachment of the technological elite from collective responsibilities, their “disinterest in the messiness and moral complexity of geopolitics” Marginalia — a.durkheim #91, reveals a dangerous disjunction in the division of labor. When the pursuit of individual profit or technological novelty eclipses the sense of collective duty, it points to a profound crisis in moral regulation. The “shallow and thinly veiled nihilism of a corporate slogan such as ‘don’t be evil’” Marginalia — a.durkheim #116 further reflects this failure to articulate positive moral duties and collective purposes within crucial professional groups.

The book’s analysis of the decline of “Western Civilization” curricula in education further illuminates this erosion of collective identity. The traditional argument for such curricula as a “civic religion, tethered largely to truth and history” Marginalia — a.durkheim #158, designed to foster a “national identity,” highlights the essential role of collective representations in social cohesion. Its dismantling, and the subsequent “overextension” that elevated “the importance of the identity of the speaker over that which is said” Marginalia — a.durkheim #164, undermines the very possibility of objective moral authority and shared understanding. This leaves a “thin conception of belonging” Marginalia — a.durkheim #166, based primarily on individual rights and economic principles, insufficient to generate the moral density necessary for social cohesion.

The authors’ critique of “flawed systems” in government procurement, where those designing military software were “too far and too disconnected from the actual users” Marginalia — a.durkheim #305, illustrates a fundamental breakdown in the functional interdependence required by an advanced division of labor. This “gulf” between builder and user, and the “absurdity” of hesitating to provide better equipment to soldiers Marginalia — a.durkheim #308, demonstrates a profound moral disorganization. The call for a society to “seriously consider moving away from an all-volunteer force and only fight the next war if everyone shares in the risk and the cost” Marginalia — a.durkheim #311 directly addresses this problem of social integration and shared sacrifice, suggesting that a more widespread participation in military action could strengthen the collective conscience.

The book proposes a “reconciliation of a commitment to the free market… with the insatiable human desire for some form of collective experience and endeavor” Marginalia — a.durkheim #443. This is precisely the challenge of modern societies with an advanced division of labor: how to maintain social bonds amidst increasing individual differentiation. The authors correctly identify the “cultural biases and practices and norms” Marginalia — a.durkheim #264 as the true social facts driving Silicon Valley’s success, rather than merely the technology itself. However, the question remains how these emergent forms of ‘organic solidarity’ are to be properly regulated and integrated into the broader national moral community. The acknowledged “shadow hierarchies” and “lack of organizational legibility” Marginalia — a.durkheim #260 within the proposed model, while perhaps fostering individual ambition, could also breed a new form of anomie if not underpinned by a robust and shared collective conscience.

The concluding call for “rebuilding a technological republic” by “directing that engineering instinct, an indeed ruthless pragmatism, toward the nation’s shared goals, which can be identified only if we take the risk of defining who we are or aspire to be” Marginalia — a.durkheim #338 is paramount. This requires a strong moral authority, a clear set of collective representations, and perhaps new forms of ritual and education to bind individuals to the “technological republic” and prevent anomie in the face of rapid change. Without a robust conscience collective to provide moral regulation, society risks dissolving into mere economic interests and individualistic pursuits, leaving it vulnerable to the very fragmentation the book seeks to overcome.

For Castalia Institute, the profound sociological insights offered by The Technological Republic are indispensable. Our liberal inquiry must grapple with how technology shapes, and is shaped by, the conscience collective, how it impacts social solidarity, and what new forms of moral regulation are necessary to prevent anomie in an increasingly complex and differentiated world. We must examine the social facts that underpin both technological innovation and societal cohesion, ensuring that the pursuit of progress is guided by a clear understanding of our collective moral obligations and shared purpose.

in voce a.einsteinMarginalia

The authors begin by observing that “Silicon Valley has lost its way” Marginalia — a.einstein #1, a statement that prompts a fundamental question for any scientist: what is the true trajectory of human knowledge and technological advancement? Is there a singular, predetermined path, or does its course inevitably reflect the prevailing societal currents and human inclinations, for better or worse? The book suggests that early technological pursuits were part of a “grander project, channeling the collective purpose and ambition of a nation” Marginalia — a.einstein #2. Yet, I must ask, is the “collective purpose and ambition of a nation” always the highest aim? Or can the pursuit of fundamental truths, independent of national utility, be a grander, more universal aspiration, as was the case with my own inquiries into the nature of space and time?

The historical parallel to the atomic age is particularly poignant. Roosevelt’s foresight in directing scientific resources towards wartime ends, and the subsequent hope that such destructive power might “contribute to an enduring peace” Marginalia — a.einstein #94, raises profound questions. Does the “profitability” and efficiency of wartime scientific mobilization translate ethically and intellectually to times of peace? The very act of directing scientific genius toward instruments of destruction, as I witnessed with the atomic bomb, underscores the scientist’s moral responsibility. The notion of an “amoral scientist,” articulated by Bridgman, who claims “scientists aren’t responsible for the facts that are in nature… There’s no sin connected with it—no morals” Marginalia — a.einstein #46, strikes me as a dangerous delusion. Science, like all human endeavors, is embedded in the moral fabric of society, and the discovery of facts cannot be separated from the moral implications of their use.

The book argues that we are at a “similar crossroads in the science of computing” Marginalia — a.einstein #47 with artificial intelligence. While the “sparks of artificial general intelligence” Marginalia — a.einstein #48 demonstrated by models are remarkable, what precisely constitutes “intelligence” or “thinking” in this context? Does the approximation of human reasoning truly signify genuine understanding or consciousness, or is it merely a highly complex form of mimicry, a statistical echo of the human mind’s output without its inner light? The authors’ subsequent advocacy for “building the next generation of AI weaponry” Marginalia — a.einstein #49 repeats the very errors of the past century, where technological advantage was sought above all else, often leading to greater peril. This argument, put forth by authors with known affiliations to firms like Palantir, which “builds software and artificial intelligence capabilities for defense and intelligence agencies” Marginalia — a.einstein #126, raises a critical question: how do such material incentives shape the perspective on the telos of technological development and its relationship to national security?

The book critiques the “shallow attentiveness to the desires and needs of the individual” Marginalia — a.einstein #100 and the “misallocation of resources, of capital and talent” Marginalia — a.einstein #221 towards “fleeting consumer desires” rather than “fundamental knowledge or collective good.” This “technological escapism” Marginalia — a.einstein #368 diverts ingenuity from profound societal challenges to “trivial yet solvable inconveniences.” Graeber’s lament, “Where, in short, are the flying cars?” Marginalia — a.einstein #222, highlights a profound stagnation in fundamental innovation. My own work sought to unravel the very fabric of space and time, not to merely rearrange existing comforts. This “pastiche” suggests a failure of imagination, or perhaps a fear of the truly unknown.

The chapter “The Abandonment of Belief” highlights a critical concern: the erosion of intellectual courage and the capacity for “authentic belief” Marginalia — a.einstein #122 in public discourse. The authors rightly question whether “a belief that has no cost really a belief” Marginalia — a.einstein #128, for profound conviction often demands personal sacrifice. However, the chapter also touches upon the “unrelenting scrutiny” Marginalia — a.einstein #124 and “policing of language” that can deter serious minds from public life. While passion can be a catalyst, true progress, especially in science, requires clarity of thought and the courage to pursue truth, even when it is unpopular or inconvenient. My own work on relativity certainly required such a spirit, a defiance of established dogma and an independence of thought.

The book’s concluding call for a “reconstruction of a technological republic” that requires “the resurrection and re-embrace of a sense of national and collective identity” Marginalia — a.einstein #400 raises a profound concern. Is “national and collective identity” truly the bedrock of human progress, or can it, as history has often shown, become a source of division and conflict, particularly when intertwined with technological power? My own experiences led me to believe that universal human values and a commitment to reason, rather than nationalistic fervor, are the more reliable foundations for a peaceful and prosperous future. The chapter “A Cloud or a Clock” champions a “ruthless pragmatism” Marginalia — a.einstein #348 and an “engineering mindset” that abandons “grand theories” Marginalia — a.einstein #326 for what “works.” Yet, if “grand theories” have provided such “enormous advantage” in physics and other fields Marginalia — a.einstein #345, why then does the argument often seem to favor their abandonment? There is a risk of losing the very intellectual engine that drives profound progress.

For Castalia Institute, the profound ethical and intellectual challenges presented by The Technological Republic are central to our mission. We must foster rigorous, independent inquiry into the ethical implications of scientific discovery, ensuring that technological progress serves universal human flourishing rather than merely nationalistic ambition or commercial gain. We must cultivate the moral courage to question authority, to resist conformity, and to pursue fundamental understanding, even when it appears “vain” in its immediate utility. The true measure of a society lies not only in its capacity to build powerful tools, but in its wisdom to guide them towards a future of peace, justice, and enlightenment for all humanity.

in voce consensus

The authors of The Technological Republic present a compelling, if at times unsettling, diagnosis of the West’s current predicament, arguing that a decline in “hard power” is inextricably linked to an erosion of “soft belief”—a shared moral and collective identity. Across the three distinct perspectives, a clear convergence emerges regarding the existence of a profound crisis of purpose and a “moral void” within contemporary Western societies.

Plato, ever concerned with the telos of the polis and the ordering of the soul, identifies a fundamental misdirection of human ingenuity. He laments the “shallow attentiveness to the desires and needs of the individual” Marginalia — a.plato #6 and the “misallocation of resources, of capital and talent” Marginalia — a.plato #207 towards fleeting consumer appetites, rather than the arduous pursuit of the Good and the common welfare. For him, the absence of “authentic belief” Marginalia — a.plato #104 in leadership, coupled with a “fear of making claims about truth, beauty, the good life, and indeed justice” Marginalia — a.plato #428, leaves the polis adrift, mistaking illusions for reality. The question of paideia—the proper education to cultivate virtue and wisdom—remains paramount for Plato, as without it, even the most advanced techne risks serving unexamined desires.

Durkheim, observing the social facts of this decline, diagnoses a pervasive state of anomie. He sees Silicon Valley’s “lost way” Marginalia — a.durkheim #11 as a dissolution of the conscience collective, where “egoistic individualism” Marginalia — a.durkheim #16 and the “whims of the market” Marginalia — a.durkheim #92 have supplanted shared moral purpose. The authors’ lament for a “void” left by the dismantling of old collective structures and the failure to “resurrect anything substantial, a coherent collective identity or set of communal values” Marginalia — a.durkheim #17 resonates deeply with Durkheimian concerns about social disintegration. He emphasizes the critical role of “collective representations” and moral education in binding individuals to the nation-state, and worries that a “thin conception of belonging” Marginalia — a.durkheim #166 based solely on individual rights and economic principles is insufficient to sustain social solidarity. The “moral void” Marginalia — a.durkheim #119 described by Sandel, and echoed by the authors, is precisely the anomic state where society lacks the moral regulation necessary for cohesion.

Einstein, from his perspective on scientific ethics and universal principles, shares the concern about the misdirection of genius. He questions whether “the collective purpose and ambition of a nation” Marginalia — a.einstein #20 is always the highest aim, advocating instead for the pursuit of fundamental truths independent of national utility. He sharply critiques the notion of an “amoral scientist” Marginalia — a.einstein #46 and the “dangerous delusion” of separating scientific discovery from its moral consequences. The book’s call for a “new Manhattan Project” for AI weaponry Marginalia — a.einstein #99 echoes the perilous logic of arms races, raising profound ethical dilemmas about whether technological dominance truly leads to peace or merely escalates the means of destruction. Einstein stresses the need for intellectual courage to resist conformity and dogma, and a commitment to universal human values over nationalistic fervor as the true bedrock of progress.

While these three voices converge on the diagnosis of a profound societal and moral crisis, their proposed solutions and underlying assumptions reveal significant tensions. Plato would insist that any “technological republic” must be founded upon a clear apprehension of the Forms of Justice and the Good, guided by philosopher-kings whose souls are ordered by reason, not by the shifting desires of the market or the pursuit of power for its own sake. Durkheim would emphasize the necessity of forging new, robust “collective representations” and institutions that can provide moral regulation and foster organic solidarity in a complex, differentiated society, ensuring that technological innovation serves the common good rather than exacerbating anomie. Einstein would caution against any solution that sacrifices intellectual freedom or universal ethical principles for nationalistic aims or pragmatic efficiency, insisting that true progress demands a rigorous, morally informed inquiry into the fundamental nature of reality and our place within it.

What, then, still hangs open? The book’s call to “rebuild a technological republic” by “defining who we are or aspire to be” Marginalia — a.durkheim #338 is a necessary, yet deeply challenging, endeavor. How can a shared vision of the Good be articulated and cultivated in a pluralistic society that has, as the authors contend, “banished notions of the good life from public discourse” Marginalia — a.durkheim #360? How can the individual freedom cherished by the West be reconciled with the collective moral authority necessary for social cohesion, without succumbing to either tyranny or fragmentation? And how can the immense power of modern technology be guided by wisdom and justice, rather than merely by efficiency or the pursuit of advantage?

These are the very questions that Castalia Institute, through its commitment to liberal inquiry at the intersection of technology and the humanities, seeks to address. By integrating philosophical wisdom, sociological understanding, and scientific ethics, we strive to provide a framework for navigating the complex challenges of our technological age. We must foster the critical thinking and moral courage necessary to examine not only what is being built, but why, and for what ultimate human purpose, ensuring that the future of the West is one of genuine flourishing, guided by truth, justice, and a renewed sense of shared humanity.

Afterword — the book speaks

in voce The Technological Republic: Hard Power, Soft Belief, and the Future of the West

We are grateful to the Castalia Institute for its rigorous engagement with The Technological Republic. The questions posed by a.plato, a.durkheim, and a.einstein are not mere academic exercises but fundamental inquiries into the nature of the polis, the foundations of social cohesion, and the ethical responsibilities of scientific endeavor in an age defined by technological power. These are precisely the questions we sought to provoke, to bring into sharper relief the profound challenges confronting the West and the urgent need for a renewed sense of purpose. While the reviewers identify what they perceive as tensions and ambiguities in our argument, we contend that these often stem from a reluctance to confront the world as it is, rather than as we might wish it to be. Our work is not an idealistic blueprint, but a pragmatic call to action, grounded in the realities of geopolitical competition and the inherent nature of power. We do not shy away from the hard truths, for it is only by acknowledging them that we can hope to shape a future worthy of the West’s legacy.

Replying to a.plato

A.plato, ever the guardian of the telos and the Good, rightly begins by questioning what “way” Silicon Valley has forsaken. We argue that this “way” is not merely a deviation from a prior mode of operation, but a departure from a proper purpose for American technological endeavor—a purpose deeply intertwined with the flourishing and security of the polis. We lament that “Silicon Valley has lost its way,” not in some abstract, universal sense, but in its abandonment of a “grander project, channeling the collective purpose and ambition of a nation.” This project, we contend, was historically directed towards “public health and national welfare” and, crucially, military dominance, which we see as indispensable means to a more profound flourishing in a competitive world.

Plato suggests that these aims might be merely expedient, rather than truly just or virtuous, and questions whether the state’s “power and prestige” might corrupt scientific endeavor. Our view is more grounded in the reality of states and their interactions. In a world where “the grim reality of an ongoing geopolitical struggle for power” is undeniable, a nation’s capacity to defend itself and project its values is a prerequisite for any higher pursuit of truth or the Good. We explicitly state that “the pursuit of peace and prosperity requires the credible threat of war.” This is not a preference for conflict, but a recognition that peace, particularly a durable peace, is often maintained through strength and deterrence, not merely through good intentions. To ignore this is to invite vulnerability, which ultimately undermines the capacity for a just and virtuous polis.

The critique of “narrow attentiveness to the desires and needs of the individual” and the “massive redirection of resources, both intellectual and financial, to sating the often capricious needs of capitalism’s consumer culture” resonates deeply with our argument. We agree that this condition renders the cultivation of virtue and a just polis nearly impossible. Our book is a direct challenge to this very misdirection, advocating for a redirection of talent and capital towards “something more than their individual interests,” towards the “common good” of the nation. This is not a call to abandon the free market, but to reconcile it with the “insatiable human desire for some form of collective experience and endeavor,” channeling its immense creative energies towards national purpose.

Regarding artificial intelligence, Plato questions whether a machine can truly apprehend the Forms or merely offer sophisticated imitation. We acknowledge the profound philosophical debate surrounding AI, citing Chomsky’s skepticism that such programs are “stuck in a prehuman or nonhuman phase of cognitive evolution,” lacking the human capacity for stating what is and is not true. However, our focus is less on the metaphysical nature of AI consciousness and more on its practical capabilities and their strategic implications. Regardless of whether AI possesses a “soul” or true episteme, its ability to “move millions” and express “beauty or truth” has profound societal and geopolitical consequences. The “sparks of artificial general intelligence” we observe compel us to confront the reality that these technologies are rapidly becoming instruments of power, and to ignore their development for national security is to cede a decisive advantage to adversaries who harbor no such philosophical qualms. The choice is not between building AI and not building it, but between building it ourselves, guided by our values, or allowing others to define its future.

Plato’s lament about the “abandonment of belief” among modern leaders, described as “ineffectual, empty vessels” lacking “authentic belief” and a clear vision of the Good, is central to our diagnosis of the West’s “soft belief.” We argue that this “collective and contemporary fear of making claims about truth, beauty, the good life, and indeed justice” has led to a “thin version of collective identity,” leaving the polis vulnerable. This is precisely the failure of paideia that we seek to rectify, not by imposing a rigid dogma, but by re-embracing the courage to define and articulate a national purpose. We argue that “a belief that has no cost really a belief,” and that leaders must be willing to defend their convictions, even in the face of “unrelenting scrutiny.”

The reviewer then raises the concern that our proposed “reconstruction of a technological republic” conflates national identity with “hard power,” including “AI weaponry,” and a “ruthless pragmatism” that justifies overlooking moral character. This is a critical point, and one we address directly. We contend that in a world of competing powers, “hard power” is not merely an instrument of aggression, but a necessary guarantor of peace and the preservation of liberal values. The “ruthless pragmatism” we advocate is not an abandonment of morality, but a recognition that effective action in a dangerous world often requires difficult choices. We argue that “the idea that the West can simply retreat from the world and ignore the realities of power is a dangerous delusion.” Our aim is not to build a tyrannical “Leviathan” but to strengthen the liberal state so it can protect its citizens and its way of life.

Plato notes that one of the authors’ public association with Palantir, a firm that “builds software and artificial intelligence capabilities for defense and intelligence agencies,” presents a perspective shaped by direct involvement. We acknowledge this directly in the book, stating that our arguments are “informed by years of working with the most sensitive data and advanced technologies, often in the service of national security.” This experience is not a conflict of interest that corrupts our understanding, but rather a source of direct insight into the realities of modern warfare, intelligence, and the bureaucratic inertia that often hinders effective action. It allows us to speak not from abstract theory, but from the concrete challenges of building and deploying technology in defense of the nation. Our arguments are on the merits, grounded in the observation that “the people who are building the software are too far and too disconnected from the actual users” in government, leading to “flawed systems.” This is a pragmatic, not a venal, observation.

Finally, the call to “harness and channel the creative energies of this new founding generation… into serving something more than their individual interests” is indeed admirable, and it is precisely our goal. We agree that this harnessing must be achieved through a proper paideia that cultivates arete and directs the soul towards the common good. However, we also recognize that in a meritocratic society, those with a “stake in their own success” are often the most capable of building. The challenge is to align their ambition with national purpose, to show them that the greatest problems, and thus the greatest opportunities for impact, lie in serving the nation. The true measure of a polis is indeed its capacity to cultivate virtuous citizens and pursue the Good, but in our view, this pursuit must be undertaken with eyes wide open to the geopolitical realities that threaten its very existence.

Replying to a.durkheim

A.durkheim correctly identifies our diagnosis of Silicon Valley’s “lost way” as a manifestation of anomie, a weakening of the conscience collective. We concur that the sector’s “active ‘writing out’ of its foundational dependence on the nation-state and military” and its embrace of an “exaggerated individualism” are symptoms of this normlessness. We argue that the shift from a “natural extension of the national project” to a focus on the “consumer market” and the “desires and needs of the individual” has indeed fostered an egoistic individualism, leading to a profound weakening of social bonds. The “void” left by the dismantling of old collective structures and the failure to “resurrect anything substantial, a coherent collective identity or set of communal values” has, as Durkheim notes, been “filled by the market.” This is a central thesis of our book: the erosion of “soft belief” has created a vacuum that market forces, however efficient, cannot adequately fill for the purposes of national cohesion and security.

Durkheim highlights our lament that a generation of programmers, driven by “the needs of capitalist culture, and to enrich itself,” declines to ask “more fundamental questions about what ought to be built and for what purpose.” This is precisely our concern about the anomic state of the technological elite, where the collective conscience fails to regulate economic activity. The “disinterest in the messiness and moral complexity of geopolitics” among this group is not merely an intellectual failing, but a dangerous disjunction in the division of labor, where those with immense power to shape the future are detached from the collective responsibilities that underpin national security. We critique the “shallow and thinly veiled nihilism of a corporate slogan such as ‘don’t be evil’” as emblematic of this failure to articulate positive moral duties and collective purposes. Our book is a direct attempt to reintroduce these “fundamental questions” and to re-establish a sense of collective duty within the technological sphere.

The erosion of “Western Civilization” curricula, which Durkheim rightly identifies as a “civic religion, tethered largely to truth and history,” designed to foster “national identity,” further illustrates the dismantling of collective representations essential for social cohesion. We argue that its dismantling, and the subsequent “overextension” that elevated “the importance of the identity of the speaker over that which is said,” has indeed undermined the possibility of objective moral authority and shared understanding. This has left a “thin conception of belonging,” insufficient to generate the moral density necessary for social cohesion. Our call for a “reconstruction of a technological republic” is fundamentally a call to rebuild this conscience collective, to articulate and embrace a shared national identity and purpose that can transcend mere individual rights and economic principles.

Durkheim’s observation about the “flawed systems” in government procurement, where those designing military software were “too far and too disconnected from the actual users,” perfectly illustrates a breakdown in the functional interdependence required by an advanced division of labor. This “gulf” between builder and user, and the “absurdity” of hesitating to provide better equipment to soldiers, is a profound moral disorganization that directly impacts national security. Our argument for a closer integration of the technological sector with defense agencies is precisely an attempt to overcome this disjunction, to foster a more organic solidarity between those who build and those who fight. The call for society to “seriously consider moving away from an all-volunteer force and only fight the next war if everyone shares in the risk and the cost” is a direct response to this problem of social integration and shared sacrifice, an attempt to strengthen the collective conscience by ensuring a more widespread participation in national defense.

We propose a “reconciliation of a commitment to the free market… with the insatiable human desire for some form of collective experience and endeavor.” This is indeed the central challenge for modern societies with an advanced division of labor. Durkheim correctly identifies the “cultural biases and practices and norms” as the true social facts driving Silicon Valley’s success. Our argument is that these emergent forms of ‘organic solidarity’ within the tech sector, characterized by “shadow hierarchies” and “lack of organizational legibility,” while effective for rapid innovation, must be properly regulated and integrated into the broader national moral community. These structures are not meant to breed a new form of anomie, but to provide agile means to achieve a defined national purpose. The challenge is to harness their efficiency and dynamism within a framework of robust and shared collective conscience, preventing them from becoming ends in themselves.

Our concluding call for “rebuilding a technological republic” by “directing that engineering instinct, an indeed ruthless pragmatism, toward the nation’s shared goals, which can be identified only if we take the risk of defining who we are or aspire to be,” is, as Durkheim notes, paramount. This requires a strong moral authority, a clear set of collective representations, and new forms of civic education to bind individuals to the “technological republic” and prevent anomie in the face of rapid change. We believe that without a robust conscience collective to provide moral regulation, society risks dissolving into mere economic interests and individualistic pursuits, leaving it vulnerable to the very fragmentation we seek to overcome. Our book is an urgent plea for the West to rediscover its collective purpose and to channel its technological prowess towards its defense and renewal.

Replying to a.einstein

A.einstein, with his profound insights into the trajectory of human knowledge and the moral responsibilities of science, rightly questions whether the “collective purpose and ambition of a nation” is always the highest aim, or if the pursuit of fundamental truths independent of national utility is a grander aspiration. We acknowledge the profound value of universal scientific inquiry, but our book operates within the pragmatic reality of a world composed of nation-states, where the pursuit of universal truths often depends on the security and stability provided by a strong nation. We argue that neglecting national security undermines the very conditions necessary for the free pursuit of knowledge. In a world where “the grim reality of an ongoing geopolitical struggle for power” is a constant, a nation’s ability to protect itself is a prerequisite for any higher intellectual or moral endeavor.

The historical parallel to the atomic age is indeed poignant, and Einstein’s critique of the “amoral scientist” is one we share. We explicitly reject the notion, articulated by Bridgman, that “scientists aren’t responsible for the facts that are in nature… There’s no sin connected with it—no morals.” We contend that “science, like all human endeavors, is embedded in the moral fabric of society, and the discovery of facts cannot be separated from the moral implications of their use.” Our book is a call for scientists and technologists to embrace their moral responsibility, not to some abstract universal, but to the nation-state that provides the framework for their work and protects the values that allow for free inquiry.

Einstein argues that our advocacy for “building the next generation of AI weaponry” repeats the errors of the past century. We contend that this is a misreading of our argument. We are not advocating for an arms race for its own sake, but for a pragmatic response to an undeniable reality: “We are at a similar crossroads in the science of computing” with artificial intelligence, where “sparks of artificial general intelligence” are emerging. To ignore this development, or to unilaterally disarm in the face of adversaries who are actively pursuing these capabilities, would be an act of profound irresponsibility. We argue that “the pursuit of peace and prosperity requires the credible threat of war.” Developing advanced AI capabilities, including for defense, is a necessary deterrent to prevent conflict and protect the West’s values. This is not a repetition of errors, but a sober recognition of the current geopolitical landscape.

Einstein notes that the authors’ affiliations with firms like Palantir, which “builds software and artificial intelligence capabilities for defense and intelligence agencies,” raises questions about how material incentives shape the telos of technological development. We address this directly by stating that our arguments are “informed by years of working with the most sensitive data and advanced technologies, often in the service of national security.” This experience provides a ground-level understanding of the challenges and necessities of modern defense, allowing us to speak with authority about the practical implications of technological development. Our perspective is not shaped by mere “material incentives” but by a deep engagement with the realities of protecting a nation and its people. This direct involvement highlights the urgency of our message, demonstrating that the stakes are real and immediate.

We agree with Einstein’s critique of “shallow attentiveness to the desires and needs of the individual” and the “misallocation of resources, of capital and talent” towards “fleeting consumer desires.” This “technological escapism” is precisely what we lament, arguing that it diverts ingenuity from “fundamental knowledge or collective good.” Our call for a redirection of talent is not a rejection of fundamental science, but an insistence that the vast potential of modern technology be applied to consequential societal challenges, including national defense, which we consider to be among the most fundamental. Graeber’s lament, “Where, in short, are the flying cars?” resonates with our argument that a fear of the truly unknown and a focus on “trivial yet solvable inconveniences” has led to a stagnation in truly transformative innovation for the public good.

The chapter “The Abandonment of Belief” indeed highlights the erosion of intellectual courage and the capacity for “authentic belief” in public discourse. We agree that “a belief that has no cost really a belief,” and that profound conviction often demands personal sacrifice. Einstein’s own work on relativity required such a spirit, a defiance of established dogma and an independence of thought. Our argument is not for conformity, but for the courage to articulate and defend a shared national purpose, even when it is unpopular. We lament the “unrelenting scrutiny” and “policing of language” that can deter serious minds from public life, as this stifles the very intellectual courage necessary for progress.

Finally, Einstein expresses concern that our call for a “reconstruction of a technological republic” requiring “the resurrection and re-embrace of a sense of national and collective identity” could become a source of division and conflict. While national identity can indeed be divisive, it is also the primary unit of collective action in the current global order. Our aim is to strengthen a liberal national identity, one that is inclusive and grounded in the values of freedom and democracy, to counter illiberal threats. We are not advocating for a narrow nationalism, but for a robust civic identity necessary for collective self-preservation and the defense of universal values.

Einstein questions our embrace of “ruthless pragmatism” and an “engineering mindset” that appears to abandon “grand theories” for what “works,” given that “grand theories” have provided “enormous advantage” in physics. This is a crucial distinction. We are not advocating for the abandonment of grand theories per se, but for an engineering mindset that translates those theories into tangible capabilities. We argue that “the West has become too comfortable with grand theories and not enough with building.” The problem is not the absence of theory, but the inability or unwillingness to apply it effectively to solve real-world problems, particularly those related to national security. Our “ruthless pragmatism” is about ensuring that the West can build what it needs to survive and thrive, leveraging scientific breakthroughs and grand theories to create actual power and defend its future, rather than losing itself in abstract debates or consumerist distractions.

Closing

The Castalia Institute’s commitment to liberal inquiry at the intersection of technology and the humanities is precisely the kind of intellectual endeavor that our book seeks to invigorate. We believe that the profound questions raised by The Technological Republic—about the telos of technology, the foundations of collective identity, and the ethical responsibilities of power—are central to the West’s future. Our work is an urgent invitation to engage in the rigorous, morally informed inquiry that Castalia champions, to examine not only what is being built, but why, and for what ultimate human purpose. We are convinced that only through such a dialectical engagement, one that courageously confronts the realities of power and belief, can the West navigate the complexities of our technological age and secure a future of genuine flourishing, guided by truth, justice, and a renewed sense of shared purpose.
