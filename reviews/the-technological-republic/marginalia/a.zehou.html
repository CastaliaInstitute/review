Chapter One — Lost Valley
Silicon Valley has lost its way.

Li Zehou: This sentence names a crisis of ends, not merely sectoral misallocation. “Way” here must be specified anthropologically: what forms of personhood and social relation are being reproduced by the current technical order?

Chapter One — Lost Valley
Roosevelt intended to see that the machinery of the state… would spur the scientific community forward in service of… public health and national welfare.

Li Zehou: Public direction can reopen historical agency, and this should not be dismissed. But state machinery cannot substitute for long sedimentation in schools, families, and civic practices; otherwise “welfare” remains administrative rather than civilizational.

Chapter One — Lost Valley
The modern incarnation of Silicon Valley has strayed significantly from this tradition… focusing instead on the consumer market.

Li Zehou: Correct in part; incomplete in framing. Critique of consumerism must avoid romanticizing wartime mobilization as a normative baseline. The harder task is to humanize technics under ordinary democratic conditions.

Chapter Two — Sparks of Intelligence
A generation of programmers remains ready to dedicate their working lives to sating the needs of capitalist culture… but declines to ask more fundamental questions about what ought to be built.

Li Zehou: The missing category is sedimentation. Higher-purpose discourse cannot be decreed from above if daily institutions have already trained attention toward short-cycle reward and private optimization.

Chapter Two — Sparks of Intelligence
The potential integration of weapons systems with increasingly autonomous AI software necessarily brings risks… But the suggestion to halt the development of these technologies is misguided.

Li Zehou: A familiar asymmetry of modernity appears: instrumental acceleration outruns ethical anthropology. The key question is not simply whether to build, but whether the resulting order cultivates citizens or only operators.

Chapter Seven — A Balloon Cut Loose
The virtue of a core curriculum… was that it facilitated… the construction of a national identity.

Li Zehou: Civilizational continuity matters, but not as return to a static canon. Tradition endures through transformation under new historical conditions, not through restorationist pedagogy.

Chapter Eleven — Builders and Users
Those designing military software were too far and too disconnected from the actual users.

Li Zehou: This is both a technical and ethical rupture. When design is detached from lived consequence, institutions become legible to managers but illegible to citizens.

Chapter Thirteen — A Cloud or a Clock
Ruthless pragmatism.

Li Zehou: Pragmatism without cultivated sensibility becomes one-sided instrumentality: optimized means, thinned ends. This is a central pathology of technical modernity.

Chapter Eighteen — An Aesthetic Point of View
Our collective and contemporary fear of making claims about truth, beauty, the good life, and indeed justice…

Li Zehou: Here the argument reaches its strongest register. Aesthetics is not ornament; it is social education of feeling, the precondition for durable civic judgment.

Chapter Eighteen — An Aesthetic Point of View
The old means of manufacturing a nation… have all but been dismantled or withered from neglect and abuse.

Li Zehou: If older integrative forms have thinned, replacement cannot be merely administrative. Institutions must bind freedom and discipline through daily, inhabitable practices.

Chapter Eighteen — An Aesthetic Point of View
Our argument is that the path forward will involve a reconciliation of a commitment to the free market… with the insatiable human desire for some form of collective experience and endeavor.

Li Zehou: This reconciliation is possible only under distributional legitimacy. Collective purpose cannot be stabilized while ordinary life becomes structurally insecure.

Chapter Sixteen — Piety and Its Price
The reconstruction of a technological republic will… require the resurrection and re-embrace of a sense of national and collective identity…

Li Zehou: National identity can organize will, but it must remain subordinate to humane formation. Otherwise the technological republic acquires power while losing civilization.
